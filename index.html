How the model works

The model is a 2D dip section: sediment enters at the landward (left) edge and is redistributed by diffusion that is fast on the coastal plain and at the shoreface, then decays with water depth offshore, which builds prograding clinoforms. Random flood pulses bring more, and sandier, sediment for a while. Together, eustasy and subsidence give relative sea level at the shoreline.

Eustasy is the sum of a long-term trend and up to three sine cycles (short, medium and long), loosely like 3rd-, 2nd- and 1st-order cycles but compressed in time for teaching. The basin is 300 km long and the section view zooms to where the sediment is (tick Show whole basin to see all of it). The far edge is open: the thin mud film that creeps along the deep basin floor passes out of the model instead of piling against a wall, and the status line reports how much left. The initial shoreline sets where sea level meets the sloping basement at the start of the run. Subsidence is zero landward of the hinge and increases linearly seaward of it; the slider sets the rate at 150 km, and the rate keeps increasing beyond that.

While relative sea level falls, rivers erode the coastal plain more efficiently, standing in for flow confined to incised valleys. The Fall-stage river erosion control sets how much stronger (1× turns it off). Stronger erosion truncates more of the older topsets beneath the sequence boundary and delivers more sediment to the falling-stage shoreline.

Waves rework the shoreface. Between the shoreline and the depth set by Wave base, marine transport pushes the bed toward an equilibrium shoreface profile that deepens seaward at the Shoreface gradient, instead of toward a flat bed. When rising sea level drowns the gentle coastal plain, it is too shallow for that profile and waves cut it down, carrying fines offshore faster than sand. That erosion sweeps landward with the shoreline and forms the wave ravinement surface (WRS), drawn for each transgression where waves removed at least 0.3 m. At a stillstand the shoreface sits at equilibrium and waves erode little. Set Wave base to 0 to turn this off.

Systems tracts follow the four-tract scheme, from relative sea level and shoreline movement: falling RSL is the FSST; rising RSL with a landward-moving shoreline is the TST; rising RSL with a seaward-moving shoreline is the LST after a fall or the HST after a transgression. Surfaces are placed at the transitions: BSFR at the onset of fall, SB at its end, TS at maximum regression and MFS at maximum transgression. An LST forms only if supply outpaces the early rise. If it cannot, the shoreline retreats as soon as sea level turns, there is no LST, and the TS sits directly on the SB. That is a valid outcome, not an error.

The section is coloured by sand content. Each model cell is about 1 km wide and covers 1,000 years, so by default colours are blended along beds (inverse-distance-squared weighting, measured in model columns and layers so it does not blend across bedding); untick Smooth along beds to see the raw model cells. The Wheeler diagram shows the same run as distance against time, and only the sediment that is actually preserved, coloured by sand content. Blank areas are gaps in the record, whether nothing was deposited or it was eroded later. Time is grouped into steps of at least 3,000 years. Grain size is carried by the sediment itself: sand, silt and clay are moved separately, each bed records how much of each it holds, erosion takes grains from the bed surface in proportion to what is there, and eroded grains keep their size when they are deposited again. Clay travels farthest offshore, sand least. The model saves one layer per thousand years, so very thin beds such as a few-centimetre ravinement lag are averaged into the deposits around them. Limitations: a single dip profile cannot separate incised valleys from interfluves, so sediment that would fill valleys is spread across the whole coastal plain. This is a simplified teaching model inspired by sedflux (Hutton & Syvitski, 2008), not sedflux itself.
